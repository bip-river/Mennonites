Rebaptizers An exhibit

Rebaptizers

Nearly everything you picture when you hear Mennonite is a photograph of a small minority.

There are about 2.1 million baptized Anabaptist-Mennonite believers in 86 countries. Around 45% of them are in Africa. Most drive cars. Most are not white. This is an exhibit in twelve parts about who they actually are, where they came from, and why they kept leaving.

Chapter One · Zurich, 1525

A bucket of water, and a death sentence

On a January night in a house on the Neustadtgasse, a handful of young men poured water over each other's heads. It was, by the law of every state in Europe, a capital crime.

Europe in 1525 was in the third year of the Reformation. Martin Luther had broken with Rome. Huldrych Zwingli was remaking Zurich. But a circle of Zwingli's own students — Conrad Grebel, Felix Manz, and a former priest named George Blaurock — had read further than their teacher was willing to go.

Their objection was simple and enormous. The New Testament, they said, shows adults choosing baptism after hearing the gospel. It shows nobody baptizing a baby. If baptism means anything, it means a decision — and an infant cannot decide.

On 21 January 1525, Blaurock asked Grebel to baptize him. Grebel did. Blaurock then baptized the others. Their enemies called them Anabaptists — "re-baptizers" — a name they rejected, because they didn't think their infant baptisms had counted in the first place.

Why this was treason, not theology

In 1525, church and state were one body. Infant baptism was the registry: it made you a Christian and a subject at the same moment. To refuse it for your child was to declare that the state's authority stopped at the church door — that a person could belong to a community the magistrate did not control. That is why the punishment was not excommunication but death. The Holy Roman Empire made rebaptism a capital offence by imperial mandate in 1529, and Catholics and Protestants enforced it with equal enthusiasm.

Within two years, Grebel was dead of plague, and Manz had been drowned in the Limmat river — a punishment his executioners called, with some wit, a third baptism. He was the first Anabaptist killed by Protestants.

The name that stuck came later

"Mennonite" is not a Swiss word. It comes from Menno Simons (c. 1496–1561), a Dutch Catholic priest in Friesland who left the church in 1536 and spent twenty-five years as a hunted fugitive, writing and organising the peaceful wing of a movement that had nearly destroyed itself. He didn't found it. He salvaged it — and it took his name.

Chapter Two · Schleitheim, 1527

Seven articles, written on the run

Two years in, the movement was leaderless, hunted, and splintering toward violence. A gathering in a village on the Swiss–German border produced the document that decided what Anabaptism would be.

The Schleitheim Confession, agreed on 24 February 1527 and largely drafted by a former Benedictine prior named Michael Sattler, is short — seven articles — and it reads less like a creed than a set of instructions for a body under siege.

The seven articles, in plain terms

1. Baptism — only for those who have repented and asked for it. Not infants.
2. The ban — members who fall into serious sin are warned twice in private, then put out of the fellowship. No violence, no magistrate: the only sanction is exclusion.
3. Breaking of bread — communion is for the baptized, gathered, committed body.
4. Separation — withdraw from "the world," meaning its churches, its taverns, its coercion.
5. Pastors — chosen by the congregation, supported by it, and replaced by it if they fall.
6. The sword — the state may use force; the Christian may not. No soldiering, no serving as a magistrate, no lawsuits, no vengeance.
7. The oath — do not swear. Not in court, not to a king. Let your yes be yes.

Article six is the one the world remembers. It is the origin of the Mennonites as a peace church — the doctrine usually called nonresistance: not merely opposing war, but declining to use coercive force at all, including in self-defence. Article seven made them legally unusable, since a subject who won't swear an oath cannot testify, enlist, or hold office.

Together they built a people deliberately, permanently outside the machinery of the state. That was the point. It is also why they spent the next four hundred years packing.

Sattler was tried at Rottenburg three months later. His tongue was cut out, his flesh torn with red-hot tongs, and he was burned. His wife Margaretha was drowned eight days after. May 1527

And what do they believe now?

Most Mennonite bodies today work from the Confession of Faith in a Mennonite Perspective (1995), twenty-four articles. It is called a confession, not a creed, and the distinction is load-bearing: a creed is fixed and a confession is a statement the church makes now and may restate later. There is no Mennonite authority that could enforce one anyway.

The recurring commitments, stripped of theological vocabulary:

Jesus is the lens. The whole Bible is read through him — the route by which the tradition reconciles the Old Testament with an absolute refusal of violence.
Following, not merely believing. The German word is Nachfolge. Salvation shows up as a changed life or the claim is empty.
The church decides together. No bishop hands down a ruling. The congregation discerns — slowly, and often badly, but together. They call it binding and loosing, from Matthew 18.
Everyone is a priest. No sacred class. It is why nobody conducts the singing.
Peace is not a policy position. It is a description of what the church is for.
Simplicity, and the refusal to swear. Both still hold, in wildly varying strictness.

That is the mainstream. The Mennonite Brethren and the huge global-south churches are considerably more evangelical in temperature. Old Order Mennonites articulate doctrine considerably less; in those communities it is carried in practice rather than stated.

A second founding document

The Dordrecht Confession (1632), written by Dutch Mennonites, is longer and gentler, and it is the one most North American groups actually adopted, in 1725. It added footwashing, and it spelled out shunning — the practice that would later split the Amish off. You'll find both texts in the Reference drawer.

Chapter Three · The Martyrs Mirror

The man who turned back

If one story explains why Mennonites are the way they are, it is a story about a frozen pond, told and retold for four hundred and fifty years.

In 1569, a Dutch Anabaptist named Dirk Willems escaped from prison in Asperen and ran across a frozen canal. He was thin from imprisonment and the ice held him. His pursuer — a "thief-catcher" employed by the authorities — followed, and went through.

Willems turned around and pulled him out.

The man he had just saved arrested him. He was burned at the stake shortly after, on a windy day, which meant the fire drew away from him and he died slowly.

Why this story does so much work

It is not a story about courage under fire. It is a story about a man who had a chance to let an enemy die and chose not to, at the certain cost of his own life. It is nonresistance made concrete, and it is the single most reproduced image in Mennonite culture — engraved, quilted, painted on barns, printed in hymnals, argued over in seminaries. When a Mennonite pacifist is asked "but what would you actually do," Dirk Willems is the answer they are reaching for.

The story survives because of a book. The Bloody Theater, or Martyrs Mirror, compiled by Thieleman J. van Braght and published in Dutch in 1660, runs to more than a thousand pages of trial records, letters from prison, and accounts of some 800 Anabaptist executions. The 1685 edition carried 104 copper engravings by Jan Luyken. For centuries it sat beside the Bible in Mennonite and Amish households — in many plain homes it still does.

The persecution was real and it was enormous. Estimates of Anabaptists executed in the sixteenth century run from about 2,500 to over 4,000, in an era when the whole movement numbered in the tens of thousands.

The chapter this leaves out

Not every early Anabaptist was a pacifist. In 1534–35 a militant faction seized the city of Münster, declared it the New Jerusalem, instituted polygamy and forced rebaptism, and was crushed with extraordinary brutality. The leaders' bodies were hung in cages from St Lambert's tower; the cages are still there. Münster poisoned the word "Anabaptist" across Europe for generations, and it is a large part of why Menno Simons spent his life insisting, in print, that his people were not those people.

Chapter Four · Five centuries of leaving

The great dispersal

No other Protestant tradition has moved this much. The reason is structural: a people who will not swear oaths, will not soldier, and will not send their children to the state's schools are a people who are always, eventually, asked to leave.

The two streams

Nearly all "ethnic" Mennonites descend from one of two flows, and they are genuinely different peoples who happen to share a theology.

The Swiss–South German stream fled Zurich and Bern for the Palatinate and Alsace, then sailed to Pennsylvania from 1683. They speak Pennsylvania Dutch. They gave us the Amish, the Old Order Mennonites, and Lancaster County.

The Dutch–North German stream fled Flanders for the Vistula delta near Danzig, drained its marshes for two centuries, then accepted Catherine the Great's invitation to Russia in 1789. They speak Plautdietsch. They gave us the Mennonite Brethren, the Kansas wheat belt, the Manitoba colonies, and — after 1922 — the colonies of Mexico, Paraguay, Bolivia and Belize.

If a Mennonite tells you their surname is Yoder, Stoltzfus or Hershberger, that's the Swiss stream. Friesen, Wiebe, Dyck, Penner, Klassen, Toews — Dutch.

What the map cannot show you

The single largest reason there are Mennonites in Ethiopia, Congo, India, Indonesia and Tanzania is not migration at all. It is mission — beginning in Java in 1851, India in 1899, Congo in 1911, Tanzania in 1934, Ethiopia in 1948. Those churches then grew on their own, fast, and are now the majority of the tradition. The arcs on this map are a story about Europeans. The next chapter is not.

Chapter Five · The centre of gravity moved

Mennonites are mostly African

This is the fact that reorganises everything else. If your mental image is a buggy on a Pennsylvania road, you are looking at a rounding error.

Mennonite World Conference counts about 2.1 million baptized believers in 86 countries across the wider Anabaptist family. Roughly 45% are in Africa. About 13% are in Asia and the Pacific, 10% in Latin America, 29% in North America — and just 3% in Europe, where the whole thing started.

Among the churches that actually belong to MWC, about 84% of members are African, Asian or Latin American.

The largest Mennonite church in the world is Ethiopian

Meserete Kristos Church — "Christ is the Foundation" — began with a Lancaster Mennonite mission in the 1940s and planted its first congregation in Addis Ababa in 1951. Under the Derg it was banned in 1982; its buildings were seized and its leaders imprisoned. It went underground and met in homes. When the ban lifted in 1991 it had grown by a factor of roughly ten. It now reports something like 370,000 baptized members in over 1,100 congregations, and it is larger than every North American Mennonite denomination combined.

The six countries with more than 100,000 are the United States, Ethiopia, India, the Democratic Republic of the Congo, Canada and Indonesia — in that order. Ethiopia passed India for second place in the late 2010s and is still climbing.

Two ways of counting, and they disagree

MWC counts baptized members of member churches. That undercounts two large populations badly. First, children: plain communities have very high birth rates and nobody is baptized before their late teens, so a colony of 5,000 people might report 2,000 members. Second, the Old Colony Mennonites of Mexico, Bolivia and Paraguay largely don't join MWC at all — so a group of perhaps 100,000 people in Mexico appears in the official census as around 34,000. Every number on this page is a floor, not a ceiling, and the gap is not evenly distributed.

Chapter Six · Sorting the tradition

The spectrum that isn't one

There is no Mennonite pope, no synod, no central authority of any kind — only several hundred independent bodies. They are usually described as sitting on a line from most traditional to most modern. They don't. Pick a trait and watch them reorder.

——

Why this isn't a slider

Compare two traits. On Power & internet, the Groffdale Conference sits at one end and Meserete Kristos at the other — about as far apart as the tradition goes. Now switch to Growth. Both jump to the top together, while Mennonite Church USA and the Dutch Doopsgezinden fall to the bottom. Groups that share nothing culturally are twinned; groups that look alike from outside are split.

Switch to Property and one body leaves the field entirely. The Hutterites hold everything in common — the largest single difference in this table, and one that a plainness ranking cannot see at all.

A single line would have to choose one trait and pretend it governs the others. None of them does.

The variable that is not in the table

Ten of these bodies descend from Swiss or Dutch Mennonite families, and membership largely tracks surname, language and lineage. Meserete Kristos and the Communauté Mennonite au Congo are convert churches, built by mission and evangelism within living memory.

That distinction accounts for much of the reordering. A convert church has no reason to be plain, because plain dress marks an inherited ethnicity it does not have. It has considerable reason to be theologically strict, because its members joined deliberately, as adults — which is the thing the whole tradition started over in 1525.

The word to know: Ordnung

Not "rules." The Ordnung is the settled, mostly unwritten understanding of how a particular congregation lives — what you wear, what you drive, what technology you accept, what you do at a funeral. It is agreed by the members, it varies from church to church a few miles apart, and it changes, slowly. Two Old Order congregations can differ over whether the buggy may have a windshield. This is not pedantry to them; it is the ongoing, negotiated question of where the line between the church and the world is drawn this year.

Chapter Seven · The dividing

The family tree

    The split that is happening right now

    Mennonite Church USA was assembled in 2002 out of the two great American bodies. It began coming apart almost immediately, over the inclusion of LGBTQ people.

    In 2015 the delegates voted both to retain the membership guidelines forbidding same-sex marriage and to grant forbearance to congregations that did not follow them. Conferences began to withdraw. At the end of 2017 Lancaster Conference — the oldest and largest, with roots in the 1710s — withdrew. Membership fell by roughly a third in a decade.

    In 2022 the delegates retired the membership guidelines entirely and passed a resolution of repentance toward the people the church had harmed. More conferences left.

    And here is the part that makes it more than a North American story: the majority of the world's Mennonites are now African, Asian and Latin American, and those churches are overwhelmingly on the traditional side of this question. Mennonite World Conference therefore holds together member churches that differ sharply on the question. The tree above shows the recurring shape of such disputes — how strictly the line is to be held, and who has standing to say — going back to 1693.

    The pattern in the splits

    Read the tree and the same argument recurs about every forty years. One group says the church has grown lax and must draw a harder line against the world. Another says the church has grown cold and must be revived. The first produces Old Orders and Amish; the second produces Mennonite Brethren and the evangelical conferences. Almost nothing splits over the seven articles of Schleitheim. They split over how strictly to keep them.

    The three corrections worth carrying away

    The Amish are not a kind of Mennonite. They are a group that left the Mennonites in 1693, over a question of church discipline. The relationship is sibling, not parent–child. Confusingly, some groups — Beachy Amish-Mennonites — later moved back toward the middle and carry both names.

    Hutterites are not "very strict Mennonites." They're a separate 1528 branch, and the thing that defines them is not plainness but communism: all property is held by the colony. A Hutterite farmer may drive a $600,000 combine he does not own.

    Most Mennonites are none of the above. They are Ethiopian Pentecostal-inflected evangelicals, Congolese pastors, Indian Telugu-speaking Mennonite Brethren, Javanese congregations older than most American ones, and Winnipeg accountants.

    Chapter Ten · The women

    The tongue screw in the ashes

    Roughly a third of the Anabaptists executed in the sixteenth century were women — an extraordinary proportion for the period, and a direct consequence of the doctrine. If baptism requires a personal decision, then a woman's decision counts, and she can be burned for it in her own right rather than as her husband's property.

    On 6 October 1573, Maeyken Wens was burned in the market square at Antwerp. She had been arrested in April, imprisoned in the Steen, and tortured; she did not recant. The court ordered that her tongue be fastened with an iron screw before she was brought out, so that she could not address the crowd on the way. It was a routine precaution with Anabaptist prisoners, who commonly spoke to onlookers from the scaffold.

    Her son Adriaen, about fifteen, came to watch, carrying his three-year-old brother Hans on his arm. When his mother was tied to the stake he fainted. He came round to find the crowd gone and the fire burnt out, went to the ashes, and searched them until he found the screw that had held her tongue. He kept it.

    The tongue screw

    The screw was kept by the family and has been preserved for more than four hundred and fifty years. Jan Luyken's engraving of Adriaen searching the ashes appears in editions of the Martyrs Mirror and is among the most widely reproduced images in Mennonite culture.

    What survives of these women is not doctrine but letters. Maeyken's farewell note to her children, written on 5 October, is preserved in the Amsterdam Mennonite archives — the only surviving letter in a martyr's own hand. She asks Adriaen to embrace his little brother now and then on her behalf.

    Anna Jansz was drowned at Rotterdam in 1539. On the way to her execution she carried her fifteen-month-old son Isaiah and called out to the crowd, offering money to whoever would raise him. A baker took him. She left him a letter, which was copied, circulated, and ended up in the Martyrs Mirror — where six of the letters preserved are from mothers to children they were about to leave.

    Ordination: the record

    1911. Ann Allebach is ordained by the General Conference Mennonite Church in Philadelphia — the first Mennonite woman ordained anywhere. She is never installed as a pastor of a congregation. She preaches, and does urban mission work, and that is the end of it for sixty years.

    1973. Emma Sommers Richards is ordained by Illinois Mennonite Conference to co-pastor the church at Lombard with her husband. She is the first woman actually to pastor a US Mennonite congregation. Ordination is not opened to all women in her own conference until 1982.

    1993. Mennonite churches in Congo vote to ordain women. In 2000 the Congolese Mennonite Brethren ordain their first woman; their North American counterparts do not.

    Today, Mennonite Church USA and Mennonite Church Canada ordain women without qualification. The Mennonite Brethren, the conservative conferences, the Old Orders and most global-south bodies do not. There is no central authority to settle it, and so it is not settled.

    John Howard Yoder

    Yoder (1927–1997) was the most influential Mennonite theologian of the twentieth century. The Politics of Jesus (1972) made Christian pacifism intellectually respectable far outside the Mennonite world; Stanley Hauerwas and a generation of theologians built on it.

    He also sexually abused and harassed women — students, colleagues, and women who came to him for counsel — over a period of some two decades, conduct he described in theological terms of his own devising. Complaints reached the seminary where he taught during the 1970s and 1980s. In 1991 eight women brought formal complaints; in 1992 his ministerial credentials were suspended, and after a disciplinary process lasting some four years his ordination was revoked at the end of 1995. He died in December 1997. His obituaries did not mention the abuse.

    Estimates of how many women were affected vary. A colleague acknowledged knowing of some forty. Two mental-health professionals who worked on the 1990s disciplinary process later put the figure at more than one hundred. In 2014 Mennonite Church USA commissioned the historian Rachel Waltner Goossen to document the case and the church's response; her report was published in 2015, and the seminary held a public service of confession and apology the same year.

    The sewing circles and the relief sales

    They funded the church. Mennonite women's sewing circles paid for the missions that produced the churches in India, Congo, Indonesia and Ethiopia — the churches that are now the majority of the tradition. The quilts sold at Mennonite Relief Sales — volunteer-run auctions that can raise hundreds of thousands of dollars in a weekend — still fund a large share of Mennonite Central Committee's work. The labour is unpaid, and it has been overwhelmingly women's.

    Two writers to read. Miriam Toews, raised in a Manitoba Mennonite town, writes critically about it: A Complicated Kindness (2004) on adolescence under the ban; Women Talking (2018), a novel built on the Bolivian rapes, imagining the women of the colony deciding whether to stay, fight, or leave. Rudy Wiebe's Peace Shall Destroy Many (1962) so angered the Mennonite community that he lost his editorship over it.

    Chapter Eleven · The peace witness

    What nonresistance built

    Article six of the Schleitheim Confession is one sentence. Over four centuries it has been kept, evaded and abandoned in varying measure. It has also produced a set of institutions, several of which now operate well outside the tradition that made them.

    Mennonite Central Committee, 1920

    Founded in a farmhouse in Kansas for a narrow purpose: to feed Mennonites starving in the ruined Russian colonies. It never stopped. MCC now works in some fifty countries, is funded substantially by volunteer labour and by quilt auctions, and has a habit of staying in places long after the news cameras leave. Its thrift shops — hundreds of them across North America — are a small industry.

    Civilian Public Service, 1941–47

    The United States entered the Second World War with a conscientious-objector provision negotiated largely by the historic peace churches — Mennonites, Quakers, Church of the Brethren. About 12,000 men served in Civilian Public Service instead of the military: forest work, soil conservation, and, most consequentially, staffing state mental hospitals.

    Roughly 4,500 of them were Mennonites, from a community that was a fraction of a percent of the population. What they saw in the asylums — restraint, filth, beatings, patients kept naked in barns — they documented and published. The resulting public exposure is generally credited as a significant force behind the reform of American psychiatric institutions in the late 1940s.

    Restorative justice, 1974

    In Elmira, Ontario, two young men went on a vandalism spree. A Mennonite probation officer, Mark Yantzi, made an unorthodox suggestion to the judge: rather than sentencing them in the usual way, make them meet every person whose property they had damaged, and work out with each one what they owed. The judge, improbably, agreed.

    That case is generally cited as the origin of the victim–offender reconciliation movement. The Mennonite scholar Howard Zehr turned it into a framework — his Changing Lenses (1990) is the founding text of restorative justice — and the approach has since been adopted in criminal-justice systems, schools and truth commissions in many countries. Zehr and others trace its logic to Schleitheim's second article: warn rather than punish, restore rather than exclude.

    Standing in the way, unarmed

    Christian Peacemaker Teams, founded in 1986 and now Community Peacemaker Teams, puts unarmed observers into places where violence is being done — Hebron, Iraqi Kurdistan, Colombia, Indigenous land defences in Canada. The premise, taken from the doctrine, is that the presence of a witness who will not fight and will not leave changes what people are willing to do. Members have been kidnapped and killed.

    The unresolved question

    Nonresistance has been argued over inside the tradition since 1527 and the argument is not settled. The question that recurs is what the doctrine requires when the person being harmed is someone other than oneself. Mennonites hold different positions on this, and no central authority exists to decide between them. The next chapter sets out the parts of the record that sit least easily alongside this one.

    Chapter Nine · Song and table

    What it actually sounds like

    Walk into an Old Order meetinghouse and the first thing you notice is what is absent. No organ. No piano. No guitar. No cross on the wall, no stained glass, no steeple outside. Then someone begins to sing.

    Traditional Mennonite worship is sung a cappella, and the reason is theological, not aesthetic. An instrument is a thing you buy, and a thing that gives one person a platform. The human voice is what everybody already has. In plain congregations there is no choir and no soloist — the singing is the whole body or it is nothing.

    In the oldest groups a man called the Vorsänger sets the pitch and sings the first syllable of each line alone; the congregation follows. There is no notation. The tunes live in memory, and they have been slowed down over four centuries until a single hymn from the Ausbund — the 1564 hymnal, still in use, much of it written by prisoners in the dungeon of Passau castle — can take twenty minutes to sing.

    606

    In more modern Mennonite congregations the singing is unaccompanied four-part harmony, and it is very good, because everybody has been doing it since they could stand. The emblem of this is a hymn everyone simply calls 606 — the Doxology, from its number in the 1969 Mennonite Hymnal. It has been renumbered since and nobody cares. At Goshen College it is sung at graduations and at reunions; alumni stand up wherever they are and find their part. It is about ninety seconds long. If you want to understand what "the priesthood of all believers" means as a lived thing rather than a doctrine, it is four hundred people in four parts, with nobody conducting.

    Other things you would see: footwashing, from John 13, practised twice a year in many congregations — you kneel and wash the feet of the person beside you. The holy kiss, in the plainest groups. Men and women seated on opposite sides. Sermons that run long and are preached, in Old Order churches, in German that most of the congregation does not fully speak.

    The table

    There are two Mennonite cuisines, and they map exactly onto the two migration streams. They share almost nothing.

    The Dutch–Russian table

    Zwieback (Plautdietsch: Tweebak) — the double-decker bun, a small ball of dough baked on top of a larger one. Komst borscht, cabbage soup, borrowed wholesale from Ukraine. Vereniki, cottage-cheese dumplings under cream gravy, from Ukrainian varenyky. Rollkuchen, fried dough strips, eaten with watermelon, a pairing that nobody can justify and nobody will abandon. Portzelky, New Year's fritters, descended from Dutch oliebollen — a fingerprint of the Netherlands surviving four centuries and five countries later. And Faspa: the late-afternoon Sunday meal, bread and cold meat and cheese and jam and coffee. Not a snack. An institution.

    The Swiss–Pennsylvania table

    Schnitz un knepp — dried apples, ham and dumplings. Shoofly pie, molasses and crumb. Chow-chow, pickled everything. Whoopie pies. This is the food most Americans think of as "Amish," and it is the Swiss stream's, not the Russian stream's. A Manitoba Mennonite has probably never eaten shoofly pie in her life.

    Two books that tell you what the food is for

    The Mennonite Community Cookbook (1950) preserved the recipes. Then, in 1976, Mennonite Central Committee published More-with-Less, by Doris Janzen Longacre — a cookbook whose argument was that North Americans should eat more simply so that other people could eat at all. It went through dozens of printings and sold hundreds of thousands of copies, mostly to people who had never met a Mennonite. It is probably the single most widely-read Mennonite book ever published, and it is a book about eating less.

    Meanwhile the relief sale — a spring auction of quilts, pie and furniture, held in a dozen states and provinces — raises millions of dollars a year for MCC. The quilts routinely go for four figures. The economy of the tradition runs, to a startling degree, on food and fabric.

    Chapter Twelve · Complications

    The harder parts of the record

    Four entries. Each is documented; each is argued over inside the tradition as well as outside it. They are set out here without adjudication.

    1 · The Third Reich

    Mennonites in Nazi Germany and in occupied Ukraine collaborated at rates that were, if anything, higher than the surrounding population. Ethnic-German Mennonites in Ukraine had been ruined by Soviet collectivisation and the terror of the 1930s; when the Wehrmacht arrived in 1941 many welcomed it as deliverance. Some served in the SS. Some participated in the murder of Jewish neighbours. In Germany, Mennonite leaders wrote to Hitler in 1933 pledging loyalty, and the nonresistance doctrine was quietly abandoned by most congregations. Mennonite historians began examining this record systematically in the 2010s, and the churches in the United States, Canada and Germany have since issued formal statements on it.

    2 · The colonies and the land

    The Latin American colonies were built on land that was not empty. In Paraguay's Chaco from 1927, Mennonite settlement displaced Enlhet, Ayoreo and Nivaclé peoples, who ended up as a labour force on the land they had lived on. In Bolivia, Mexico and Belize the pattern repeated. The colonies are also now among the most aggressive agricultural frontiers in South America: satellite studies of the Chaco and eastern Bolivia identify Mennonite colonies as a significant driver of deforestation, clearing hundreds of thousands of hectares of dry forest.

    3 · Manitoba Colony, Bolivia

    Between roughly 2005 and 2009, women and girls in an Old Colony settlement in Bolivia woke bruised, bleeding and unable to remember the night. They were told they were lying, or imagining, or being punished by demons. In 2009 men from the colony were caught; they had been using a livestock anaesthetic to sedate entire households and had raped at least 130 women and children, some as young as three. Nine were convicted in 2011. The case exposed how a community structured to be beyond the reach of outside authority is also beyond the reach of outside protection. It became the basis of Miriam Toews' novel Women Talking and the 2022 film.

    4 · The ban, from the inside

    Shunning is doctrine — it comes straight out of Schleitheim's second article. In its strict forms it means a family may not eat at the same table as an excommunicated son, or accept anything from his hand. It is defended as a redemptive practice: an ache designed to bring someone back. It is experienced by many of the people it lands on as the total, sanctioned destruction of every relationship they have. Ex-Old-Order and ex-Old-Colony memoirs are a small, harrowing literature. Whether the practice is a discipline or an abuse is a live argument inside the tradition, not just outside it.

    Both records belong to the same church

    Mennonite Central Committee and the Chaco colonies are the same tradition. The men who staffed the asylums rather than fight and the men who joined the SS in Ukraine were reading the same confession. Restorative justice and the Yoder case come out of the same church.

      Primary sources

      All of these are long out of copyright and available in full online. What follows is a short guide to what each one is and why it matters.

      Schleitheim Confession · 24 February 1527

      Seven articles, agreed by Swiss Brethren at Schleitheim, largely drafted by Michael Sattler. The founding constitution of Anabaptist separatism. Its full title calls it a "brotherly union" — it was written to settle arguments, not to convert outsiders.

      The seven, in order:

      1. Baptism — for those who have repented and request it
      2. The ban — private warning twice, then exclusion, before communion
      3. Breaking of bread — for the baptized body only
      4. Separation from the abomination — from state churches and worldly assemblies
      5. Shepherds — chosen and supported by the congregation
      6. The sword — ordained outside the perfection of Christ; the Christian may not wield it, nor serve as magistrate
      7. The oath — do not swear at all
      Menno Simons · c. 1539–1561

      A Frisian priest who renounced Rome in January 1536 after years of doubt about the mass and infant baptism, and after the massacre of Anabaptists — including his own brother — at the Oldeklooster. He wrote constantly, on the run, for twenty-five years, with a price on his head. His governing text, printed at the head of most of his works, was 1 Corinthians 3:11 — that no one can lay a foundation other than the one already laid, Jesus Christ.

      His major work, the Foundation of Christian Doctrine (1539), was written to distinguish his people from the militants of Münster and to argue that the true church is visible, voluntary and unarmed.

      Martyrs Mirror · Thieleman J. van Braght, 1660

      Full title: The Bloody Theater, or Martyrs Mirror of the Defenseless Christians. Over a thousand pages: trial transcripts, letters smuggled out of cells, execution accounts, running from the apostles through to Anabaptists of van Braght's own century. The 1685 second edition added 104 copper engravings by Jan Luyken, which are the visual vocabulary of Mennonite memory. A 1748–49 German edition printed at the Ephrata Cloister in Pennsylvania was the largest book produced in colonial America.

      It was compiled as a warning, not a monument: van Braght thought the Dutch Mennonites of his own prosperous day had gone soft, and he wanted them to look at what their grandparents had paid.

      Dordrecht Confession · 21 April 1632

      Eighteen articles, written by Dutch Mennonites, adopted by Alsatian Mennonites in 1660 and by North American Mennonites in 1725. It is longer, warmer and more systematic than Schleitheim, and it is the confession most traditional groups actually use. It added footwashing as an ordinance, and it set out the practice of shunning in detail — which is precisely the article Jakob Ammann would invoke in 1693 when he broke with the Swiss for not applying it strictly enough.

      The Ausbund · first printed 1564

      The oldest hymnal in continuous use by any Christian body. Many of its hymns were written by Anabaptist prisoners in the dungeon of Passau castle in the 1530s. It has no musical notation — the tunes are carried orally, sung unaccompanied, slowly, in unison, led by a Vorsänger. The Amish still use it. A single hymn can take twenty minutes.

      Mennonite Low German

      Plautdietsch

      Not a dialect of Dutch or of standard German, but a Low Prussian variety carried out of the Vistula delta in the 1780s, stiffened with Dutch and later Russian, and spoken today in Manitoba, Chihuahua, the Bolivian lowlands, the Paraguayan Chaco, Belize and Siberia. Perhaps 400,000 speakers. It is the mother tongue of the Dutch–North German Mennonite stream — a language that has never had a country.

      Spellings vary widely; there is no single standard orthography, and there never has been.

      Plautdietsch"Flat German." The language itself.
      FaspaThe late-afternoon meal, around four o'clock: bread, cold meat, cheese, jam, coffee. Not a snack and not dinner. Sunday Faspa after church is a social institution, and the word survives fluently in the mouths of people who speak no other Plautdietsch at all.
      TweebakThe double-decker bun — a small ball of dough baked on top of a larger one. Eaten at Faspa. The Dutch-stream Mennonite object par excellence.
      VerenikiCottage-cheese dumplings, boiled, drowned in cream gravy. Borrowed from Ukrainian varenyky — the loanwords track the migration exactly.
      KomstborschtCabbage borscht. Another Russian inheritance, and a fixture of the Manitoba and Kansas table.
      RollkuchenFried dough strips, eaten with watermelon. The pairing is non-negotiable and no one can fully explain it.
      PortzelkyNew Year's fritters, raisins inside. From the Dutch oliebollen — a fingerprint of the Netherlands, four centuries and five countries later.
      KjoakjChurch.
      VrintschoftKin, the extended family, the relations. In a community where a few dozen surnames circulate, this word does a great deal of work.

      The other Mennonite language

      The Swiss stream speaks something else entirely: Pennsylvania Dutch (Pennsylvania German), a Palatine German variety, spoken by Old Order Mennonites and by the Amish. "Dutch" here is a corruption of Deitsch. It is unrelated to Plautdietsch, and speakers of the two cannot understand each other. That two mutually unintelligible German languages both belong to "the Mennonites" is the clearest evidence you'll get that this is not one people.
